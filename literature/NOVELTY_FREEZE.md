# Novelty Freeze

**Stage:** Canonical Stage 6 — Novelty Re-Kill  
**Status:** PASS — narrow novelty survives

## Frozen contribution claim

The paper isolates competition over the sectoral allocation of an exogenously fixed productive-policy capacity. The capacity constraint couples sectoral contests that would otherwise be independent. Under moderate cross-regional rivalry, a sufficiently large policy capacity makes differentiated regional priorities desirable under constrained coordination while the decentralized equilibrium remains uniquely symmetric and continues to favor the same intrinsically attractive sector.

Do not expand this claim without reopening Stage 6.

## What is already known

Do not claim novelty for the following broad ideas:

- fiscal competition can distort the composition of public spending;
- regional competition can generate inefficient duplication;
- decentralized policy can differ from coordinated/centralized allocation;
- asymmetric or concentrated efficient spatial allocations can arise in public-input competition;
- linear-quadratic strategic-substitute / anti-coordination game structure is new.

## What survived the re-kill

The surviving margin is the combination of:

1. competing regional governments;
2. an exogenously fixed productive-policy capacity at the regional level;
3. sectoral allocation of that fixed capacity as the strategic choice;
4. otherwise separable sectoral contests coupled by the capacity constraint; and
5. a capacity threshold at which constrained coordination becomes strictly sectorally differentiated while the unique decentralized equilibrium remains duplicated.

## Prohibited novelty wording

Do not use:

- "first model";
- "first to show";
- "new theory of fiscal competition";
- "fiscal competition distorts composition" as the contribution;
- "regional competition causes duplication" as the contribution;
- "decentralized symmetry versus centralized concentration" as the contribution.

## Safe positioning sentence

> Existing fiscal-competition models show that decentralization can distort the level and composition of public spending and can generate inefficient spatial patterns. This paper isolates a narrower margin: competing regional governments allocate a predetermined productive-policy capacity across sectors. The fixed-capacity constraint couples sectoral contests and creates a capacity threshold at which constrained coordination calls for opposite regional sectoral orientations even though the decentralized equilibrium remains uniquely duplicated.
